Outage Chaos: Who Turned Off All The Computer?

It's been a tough couple of weeks for anyone trying to use email , fly , spend money , use an Apple device *, or do signals intelligence . Technical service outages are nothing new, but why do so many big ones seem to be happening all at once? Are hackers, AI, and international conflicts to blame? And what can we do about it? Since most service interruptions don't make the news, it's hard to get a baseline on how often we see them happen normally. But after a major incident like the Microsoft cloud outage on November 12 , any smaller disruption is more likely to become a story as part of a pattern instead of an isolated incident. We would have heard plenty about the Air Traffic Control problems anyway, but the November 18 disruption to the PlayStation Network might have gotten less attention. There might appear to be a spike or an upward trend in outages, but we can't easily verify that, and the increased coverage will tend to make it seem more significant and interconnected than it necessarily is regardless. One boring reason we might see an uptick in problems right now is the date on the calendar. With Thanksgiving travel, Black Friday, Cyber Monday, Giving Tuesday, and Surveillance Sunday (we assume) all coming up, organizations often schedule major system updates for the first three weeks of November. This helps them prepare for holiday traffic, and lets them shift the outage risk to the time before those dates when they may consider it far more important to maintain reliable service. While none of these particular outages have yet been directly attributed to AI ( or to malicious hackers ), bank CEOs and the NSA briefed congress and reportedly attributed at least some of them to rushed attempts to patch vulnerabilities found by AI. That’s understandable: all software updates carry risk, and companies and governments are having to deploy more updates, and faster, than they’ve ever needed to before. Some of those updates are to important infrastructure, where an outage would be very disruptive, but the impact of leaving vulnerabilities unpatched would be even worse. Vulnerabilities are a type of software bug that affects security. Once a vulnerability is found in a given piece of software and disclosed, typically the software maker will release a patch fixing that vulnerability.

New York and California Age Gating Rules Go Into Effect in the New Year

Come January 2027, everyday social media browsing in two of the country's largest states will look noticeably different. In California, AB 1709 (2026), AB 1043 (2025), and SB 976 (2024) are slated to take effect on January 1, 2027. Meanwhile, state attorney general regulations for New York’s Stop Addictive Feeds Exploitation (SAFE) for Kids Act take effect on January 25, 2027. EFF has opposed these laws across legislatures , regulatory proceedings , and the courts . The core problem is simple: age-gating mandates trade away everyday user privacy and free expression under the guise of safety, while pushing platforms to gather even more personal data on users. What California’s Rules Say Three interlocking California laws take effect on January 1, 2027, reshaping how platforms handle age checks and design features for young users. First, SB 976 (2024) requires verifiable parental consent before platforms can offer algorithmic feeds or nighttime notifications to anyone under 18. Second, AB 1709 (2026) goes even further by outright banning "addictive features," including personalized recommendation algorithms, autoplay, and endless scroll, for users under 16, even with parental permission. Third, the Digital Age Assurance Act (AB 1043, 2025) mandates age-assurance checks across devices (operating systems) and apps, serving as the enforcement backbone for those restrictions. Together with the California Attorney General’s proposed rules on SB 976 , which outline age-gating methods, accuracy, circumvention, and parental consent, these laws force platforms into a two-tier design that walls young people off from basic content-discovery tools, puts everyone's privacy at risk to prove their age, and has already drawn First Amendment lawsuits from social media companies against both SB 976 and AB 1709 . For example: rather than minimizing corporate surveillance and data collection by passing stronger privacy laws, the SB 976 regulations encourage companies to evaluate behavioral patterns, collected user data, digital interactions, and metadata to estimate age (Sec. 561(a)(2)), alongside location data collected for other purposes (Sec. 565). Good privacy laws should discourage collecting that information in the first place, not reward broader tracking.

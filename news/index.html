The Justice Department Wants To Give Patent Trolls More Power

Software patents are mostly vague, hard to define, and widely abused. Most tech patent lawsuits aren’t filed by companies that make real products. They’re filed by patent trolls, companies that acquire patents to extract payments through litigation and legal threats. A patent on one feature of a smartphone shouldn’t give the patent owner the power to threaten the existence of the entire product line. But that is exactly the leverage patent trolls want—and the current administration is helping them to get it. In two recent patent cases, Radian Memory Systems v. Samsung and Collision Communications v. Samsung, the Justice Department and the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office have asked courts to make it easier for patent owners to get injunctions. These are judicial orders that can force companies to stop selling products or remove features from them, rather than simply pay monetary damages. The threat of an injunction can pressure a company to pay far more than what a patent is actually worth. In 2006, NTP Inc., a patent holding company with no products of its own, was able to pressure BlackBerry manufacturer Research in Motion Ltd. to pay a $612 million settlement when it was on the verge of an injunction. Protection Against Patent Trolls In May 2006, the Supreme Court rejected the practice of granting permanent injunctions almost automatically after a finding of patent infringement. In eBay v. MercExchange , the high court held that patent owners must satisfy the same tough requirements that plaintiffs in non-patent cases must meet to get a permanent injunction. After eBay, patent owners had to show “irreparable harm,” that they can’t be adequately compensated by money, that the “balance of hardships” favors the plaintiff, and, critically, that an injunction would not harm the “public interest.” At EFF, we celebrated eBay v. MercExchange as a major win for internet users. More than twenty years ago, we saw that software patents were going to be a disaster—not just for small companies (which they are), but for freedom of expression online. The Paramount-Warner Brothers Deal Could Usher in Another Age of Lost Media

There are all sorts of reasons to be against the merger of Paramount and Warner Brothers-Discovery. There's the obvious problem of increasing concentration in the already pretty-concentrated film and TV industry. People have problems with Paramount's owner Skydance interfering with its news coverage and late night comedy . But one of the biggest is the way we are losing choice and access to information and culture. In the context of the digital world, there are two major problems with these mega mergers. One, as we've said in the past, is that it further degrades the already awful state of mainstream streaming services. In short, by increasing the amount of legacy content under their control, major studios don't have to invest in either new content or well-engineered services to get users. Plus, they can pool the data they have on users together in an increasing assault on consumer privacy. A monthly subscription and more information about your habits and interests? Win-win for them. Another problem is the disappearing of content. In the digital era, there should be no more lost media. Making and storing copies of media is easier and cheaper than it ever has been. The idea of the persistence of content online has become the adage "the internet never forgets." However, since more and more media is locked behind subscription services under the control of fewer and fewer companies—and as physical copies, or even just digital copies that are actually owned by consumers also vanish—access to our common culture is at risk. Incentives to take information and media offline should raise huge red flags. Unfortunately, they are multiplying. First, there's the impulse of merged companies to offset the cost of their merger by shelving projects and claiming them as tax write-offs. We saw this the last time Warner Brothers was taken over, with projects being ended in order to take advantage of a specific kind of tax benefit. It has little to do with the quality or even how much or how complete a project is—money is the driving factor and we as a culture are all poorer for it. And we all know it. That's why the outrage eventually led to Coyote vs Acme being released by a much smaller studio.

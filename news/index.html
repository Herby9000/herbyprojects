Traces of Justice review – ‘Chinese Sherlock Holmes’ at centre of arresting forensic cop thriller

Based on real scientist Cui Daozhi, this sharp feature has thumping set pieces and knotty puzzles set across 30 years of gripping cases The latest from Chen Sicheng, prime mover behind the hit Detective Chinatown franchise , offers a disarming mix of propaganda, copaganda and gripping narrative. It’s essentially a portmanteau biopic: its hero is Cui Xun, a fictional stand-in for the venerable Cui Daozhi , a still-extant real-life figure who so revolutionised forensic science he became known as “China’s Sherlock Holmes”. Ripping three-and-a-bit tales from the casebook – involving a 1970s child killer, a grisly 1980s multiple murder and a 1990s bus bombing (plus one lingering loose end), Chen constructs a history of forensic investigation that is also, in variably oblique ways, a history of Chinese law enforcement, which is shown evolving from boozy, gloveless crime-scene trampling to something far more streamlined and professional. Within the handsome period framework, Chen follows the science: one era’s crazy innovation becomes commonplace in the next. A rookie Cui (played here by Zhang Yi, ageing up subtly) uses boiled potatoes to take dental imprints; when these run out, he and his colleagues start biting one another’s cheeks to make their points. (Their scars are a terrific visual gag: scientists literally showing their working.) By the 90s, when Cui is heading a team working out of a vast, well-staffed warehouse, we seem mere moments away from David Caruso’s sunglasses and memorable Who guitar riffs. The forbidding subtext is presumably that, after 50 years of such breakthroughs, there’s no more getting away with it; as one suspect insists: “everyone pays for what they’ve done.” Continue reading...

Electronic Frontier Foundation · 2026-11-09 · 3 min · 660 words available

We’re Asking an Appeals Court to Require a Warrant for ALPR Searches

Today , along with the ACLU, EFF filed an amicus brief in the Sixth Circuit court of appeals in United States v. Sturdivant , a case where police warrantlessly tracked a suspect’s movements using Automated License Plate Reader (ALPR) data. Our brief details the vast, interconnected network of ALPRs that constantly track drivers around the country and argues that this tracking is a “search” under the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. Sturdivant involves a series of robberies in and around Cleveland, Ohio. Using surveillance camera footage, police identified the make, model, and color of the robber’s car, but they did not know its license plate number. After running an open-ended search of two ALPR databases, Flock and ELSAG, officers learned a car belonging to the defendant had been tracked near sites of the robberies, eventually leading to his arrest. Faced with a motion to suppress the ALPR evidence, the district court held that Mr. Sturdivant had no reasonable expectation of privacy in his license plate number because it could be observed by members of the public, and that therefore he had no Fourth Amendment interest in not having his car tracked in public places. But ALPRs are simply not comparable to observing an individual license plate on a passing car. As we’ve explained in many amicus briefs and in our lawsuits directly challenging ALPR use in San Jose, California and Wisconsin , ALPRs give law enforcement “ a virtual time machine to view suspects’, or anyone else’s, past movements (and much more) with just the click of a button.” Just last month, a federal district court agreed and held that ALPRs are a “ form of indiscriminate mass surveillance” that can violate the Fourth Amendment. We hope the Sixth Circuit will reverse the lower court in this case and hold that ALPR searches require a warrant. Related Cases: Wisconsin Council of Churches v. Milwaukee, Racine, Kenosha & Green Bay SIREN and CAIR-CA v. San Jose

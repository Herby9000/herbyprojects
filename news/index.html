Electronic Frontier Foundation · 2026-11-18 · 4 min · 872 words available

Voters Reject ALPRs and Surveillance Tech at the Ballot Box

Do automated license plate readers (ALPRs) have any place in our communities? That question has been a hot topic in city council meetings , community conversations , and news coverage around the country for months. This past election day, a couple of U.S communities took it further: to the ballot. And overwhelmingly, voters decided that, no, these devices should not be deployed on our streets. We agree! In Maine, the coastal community of York voted to remove the town’s existing ALPR cameras and require that any future proposed installations receive voter approval before proceeding. The local initiative ended up on the ballot after constituents gathered enough signatures on a citizens’ petition for a local ordinance. On Election Day, nearly three-quarters of York voters supported the ban. Thousands of miles away, voters in League City, Texas also voted against allowing the local police department to continue use of ALPR cameras. That vote was formally approved on November 17, which began a 72-hour window to remove the cameras. Since the election, the cameras have been “bagged” or covered to prevent the collection of data. At least one city also decided to expand protections afforded to their residents when it comes to the use of surveillance technology. In Pflugerville, Texas, Proposition B passed , now requiring that the City Charter be amended to include “protections governing the collection, use, retention, and oversight of data, facial recognition, and surveillance technologies.” It also requires “a responsible artificial intelligence framework.” While every municipality has different rules for how to get a local ordinance placed on the ballot, banning use and securing the removal of surveillance tools locally is a powerful way of protecting your community against devices and data collection that goes far beyond your town lines. Surveillance is a choice, not a mandate, and our communities have the power to refuse it. EFF has supported local communities around the country over the last few years in advocating against the procurement of surveillance technologies in their cities, counties, and states. These victories at the ballot box and in city councils show that when mass surveillance is presented as a policy choice, not a technical necessity, communities can and do reject it. The Justice Department Wants To Give Patent Trolls More Power

Software patents are mostly vague, hard to define, and widely abused. Most tech patent lawsuits aren’t filed by companies that make real products. They’re filed by patent trolls, companies that acquire patents to extract payments through litigation and legal threats. A patent on one feature of a smartphone shouldn’t give the patent owner the power to threaten the existence of the entire product line. But that is exactly the leverage patent trolls want—and the current administration is helping them to get it. In two recent patent cases, Radian Memory Systems v. Samsung and Collision Communications v. Samsung, the Justice Department and the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office have asked courts to make it easier for patent owners to get injunctions. These are judicial orders that can force companies to stop selling products or remove features from them, rather than simply pay monetary damages. The threat of an injunction can pressure a company to pay far more than what a patent is actually worth. In 2006, NTP Inc., a patent holding company with no products of its own, was able to pressure BlackBerry manufacturer Research in Motion Ltd. to pay a $612 million settlement when it was on the verge of an injunction. Protection Against Patent Trolls In May 2006, the Supreme Court rejected the practice of granting permanent injunctions almost automatically after a finding of patent infringement. In eBay v. MercExchange , the high court held that patent owners must satisfy the same tough requirements that plaintiffs in non-patent cases must meet to get a permanent injunction. After eBay, patent owners had to show “irreparable harm,” that they can’t be adequately compensated by money, that the “balance of hardships” favors the plaintiff, and, critically, that an injunction would not harm the “public interest.” At EFF, we celebrated eBay v. MercExchange as a major win for internet users. More than twenty years ago, we saw that software patents were going to be a disaster—not just for small companies (which they are), but for freedom of expression online.

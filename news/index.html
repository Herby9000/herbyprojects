Surveillance Isn't Safety: A Comic About Organizing Against Flock and Other Foes

In the new comic "Surveillance Isn't Safety"—available for free today— cartoonist Bryn Adams explores the growing movement against mass surveillance through their involvement in the community fight against Flock Safety in Cleveland, Ohio. It's a personal journey that will resonate widely with residents in hundreds of cities where communities are drawing the line against automated license plate readers (ALPRs). Jump to the Comic Or download the PDF Published in collaboration between EFF and Crucial Comix , Bryn's comic captures the authenticity of the grassroot backlash against the surveillance industry—undercutting Flock founder Garrett Langley's latest dubious defense that public opposition of his company's product isn't real. At the International Association of Chiefs of Police conference this fall, Langley accused the anti-surveillance activism of being driven by Russian bots and as being "inorganic." He pointed to the fact that many local groups were using similar language in their protest signs as supposed proof, when in reality, shared slogans are one of the hallmarks of a social movement. This was all misdirection from the grim realities that Flock and other ALPR systems facilitate: the mistakes are real; the abuse is real; the racism is real; and the targeting of dissenters , migrants , and abortion seekers is real. And, importantly, the cancelled contracts are real. Bryn's comic sheds light on the real abuses and threats driving communities to mobilize against Flock, Axon, and other ALPR vendors. It shows that coast-to-coast activism doesn't happen overnight—any more than a comic appears out of thin air as a finished product. As advisors on the cartoon, EFF watched how Bryn's script evolved from rough sketches to penciled drawings, then to inked and shaded panels, mirroring how the long-running movement against mass surveillance has steadily snowballed over the last few years. There is nothing “inorganic” about either: Both are built on passion, commitment, and putting in the work. Electronic Frontier Foundation · 2026-11-18 · 4 min · 874 words available

Voters Reject ALPRs and Surveillance Tech at the Ballot Box

Do automated license plate readers (ALPRs) have any place in our communities? That question has been a hot topic in city council meetings , community conversations , and news coverage around the country for months. This past election day, a couple of U.S communities took it further: to the ballot. And overwhelmingly, voters decided that, no, these devices should not be deployed on our streets. We agree! In Maine, the coastal community of York voted to remove the town’s existing ALPR cameras and require that any future proposed installations receive voter approval before proceeding. The local initiative ended up on the ballot after constituents gathered enough signatures on a citizens’ petition for a local ordinance. On Election Day, nearly three-quarters of York voters supported the ban. Thousands of miles away, voters in League City, Texas also voted against allowing the local police department to continue use of ALPR cameras. That vote was formally approved on November 17, which began a 72-hour window to remove the cameras. Since the election, the cameras have been “bagged” or covered to prevent the collection of data. At least one city also decided to expand protections afforded to their residents when it comes to the use of surveillance technology. In Pflugerville, Texas, Proposition B passed , now requiring that the City Charter be amended to include “protections governing the collection, use, retention, and oversight of data, facial recognition, and surveillance technologies.” It also requires “a responsible artificial intelligence framework.” While every municipality has different rules for how to get a local ordinance placed on the ballot, banning use and securing the removal of surveillance tools locally is a powerful way of protecting your community against devices and data collection that goes far beyond your town lines. Surveillance is a choice, not a mandate, and our communities have the power to refuse it. EFF has supported local communities around the country over the last few years in advocating against the procurement of surveillance technologies in their cities, counties, and states. These victories at the ballot box and in city councils show that when mass surveillance is presented as a policy choice, not a technical necessity, communities can and do reject it.

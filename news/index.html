Electronic Frontier Foundation · 2026-11-20 · 4 min · 800 words available

EFF to Congress: Mass Surveillance Is Too Important to Shove into Must-Pass Bill

In June 2026, the surveillance authority Section 702 of FISA expired absent a consensus among lawmakers over the terms of its reauthorization . Now, with the program set to truly turn off within the next few months, surveillance hawks and national security maximalists are rushing to renew this secretive power by shoving it into a must-pass bill, like a continuing resolution to fund the government or the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) or an end of year, rushed, funding bill to prevent a shutdown. This would be both undemocratic and suppress any real chance at reform. In an effort to prevent that, EFF has singed into a letter with over 25 civil liberties organizations, including the ACLU, Color of Change, and the Brennan Center, to urge Congress not to allow such a significant and dangerous program to be tacked onto a must-pass bill. The letter says: “The NDAA is among the most important bills Congress considers each year. It should not be used as a vehicle to circumvent or to cut short this long-running bipartisan debate over Americans’ civil liberties and constitutional rights, nor to force lawmakers to choose between supporting our national defense and ending warrantless spying on Americans. Adding language in the NDAA conference to reauthorize Section 702 that has not been fully debated and voted on would be a serious procedural and substantive failure, and a betrayal of the democratic process.” You can read the entire letter here . Section 702 grants the government authority to compel a telecommunications company to hand over data to the National Security Agency (NSA) in an attempt to protect national security by monitoring communications overseas—but this is only a piece of what the law does. Because we live in a globalized world, communications between people in other countries and in the United States often gets hoovered up. Once it does, the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) and several other agencies give themselves permission to search through U.S. persons’ data without a warrant. This is unconstitutional, undermines our right to private communications, and creates a dangerous and potent tool for would-be despots who might use that communication to discriminate, marginalize, or punish people based on their identities and political beliefs.
